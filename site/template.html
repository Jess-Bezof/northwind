Question 1 · Growth

Why it matters

Question 2 · Customer dependence

Which markets depend on just one or two customers, and how much revenue is at risk if a key customer leaves?

Why it matters

Question 1 showed that growth often rests on a few accounts. If most of a country's revenue comes from one customer, that market is only as safe as that relationship: one lost account could wipe it out. Knowing where this risk sits tells the manager where to invest in keeping key accounts, and where to look for new customers.

Approach

For each of the ${q2_n_countries} customer countries, using all orders from ${data_start} to ${data_end}, I measured total revenue, the number of customers who ordered, and the share of revenue that came from the single largest customer. I call a market dependent when its largest customer brings in ${q2_threshold} or more of the country's revenue. "Revenue at risk" is that customer's revenue: what the market would lose if they left.

Main finding

${q2_n_dependent} of ${q2_n_countries} markets depend on a single customer. Together they make up ${q2_dependent_share} of company revenue, and ${q2_total_at_risk} of that sits with their top customers.

  • ${q2_top_risk_country} is the clearest risk. It is Northwind's ${q2_top_risk_rank} market (${q2_top_risk_revenue}) but has only ${q2_top_risk_customers_word} customers, and ${q2_top_risk_customer} alone provides ${q2_top_risk_share} (${q2_top_risk_at_risk}).
  • ${q2_single_list} each have just one customer. The largest of these is ${q2_biggest_single}, where ${q2_biggest_single_customer} is worth ${q2_biggest_single_revenue}.
  • ${q2_diversified} are well diversified: ${q2_diversified_customers} customers each, and no single customer above ${q2_diversified_max_share}.

A share-based rule misses the biggest dollar exposures. ${q2_big_exposure_countries_cap} fall just under the ${q2_threshold} line, so they are not flagged. Yet they hold Northwind's two largest single-customer exposures: ${q2_big_exposures}.

${q2_table}

All dependent markets, ranked by revenue at risk.

Business implication

Treat the largest accounts as named relationships with an owner and a plan. Start with ${q2_top_risk_customer} in ${q2_top_risk_country}, and with the top customers in ${q2_big_exposure_countries}: these are the accounts whose loss would cost the most. In ${q2_top_risk_country} and the one-customer markets, winning even one more sizeable customer would make the market far less fragile. The smallest one-customer markets are a low priority: little revenue depends on them.

Caveat: dependence is a risk, not a prediction. Nothing in the data suggests any of these customers is about to leave.

Question 3 · Sales team

Do top-performing sales reps sell more because they win more orders or bigger orders, and what does that suggest for coaching?

Why it matters

Revenue per sales rep varies widely, from ${q3_revenue_span}. Whether the best reps win on volume (more orders) or on size (bigger orders) points to different coaching: prospecting and follow-up in one case, upselling in the other.

Approach

Each rep's revenue equals their number of orders times their average order value. For the ${q3_n_reps_word} sales representatives, I compared how much each of these two drivers varies across the team, and whether reps rank in the same order on each driver as they do on revenue. I left out the ${q3_n_excluded_word} employees in other roles, because they don't do the same job: ${q3_excluded}. Together they account for ${q3_excluded_share} of revenue.

Main finding

The top reps win more orders, not bigger ones. Across the team, the number of orders varies ${q3_orders_spread}× (${q3_orders_span}), while average order value varies only ${q3_aov_spread}× (${q3_aov_span}). Ranking reps by number of orders gives the same order as ranking them by revenue for ${q3_rank_match} of the ${q3_n_reps} reps; only ${q3_rank_mismatch} swap places. Order size does not follow revenue: ${q3_biggest_orders_rep}, who writes the largest orders, is ${q3_biggest_orders_rep_revenue_rank} in revenue, and the top earner, ${q3_top_rep}, has only the ${q3_top_earner_order_size_rank}-largest average order.

  • The US office (${q3_usa_reps}) handles ${q3_usa_orders_span} orders per rep. ${q3_top_rep} leads with ${q3_top_rep_orders} orders and ${q3_top_rep_revenue}.
  • The UK office (${q3_uk_reps}) handles ${q3_uk_orders_span} orders per rep. Yet ${q3_biggest_orders_reps}, both in the ${q3_biggest_orders_office} office, write the largest average orders on the team.
  • ${q3_lowest_rep} is the exception: low on volume and the smallest average order (${q3_lowest_rep_aov}).

Business implication

Coaching should focus on winning more orders, especially in the UK office. The UK reps already write large orders, so the opportunity is to win more of them: more prospecting, more follow-up with existing customers, and learning from how the US reps generate volume. ${q3_lowest_rep} would also benefit from help on order size. Revenue rankings should not be read as a measure of individual skill on their own (see the caveat).

Caveat: the office split also lines up with territory and seniority. The US reps were hired ${q3_usa_hired_span} and the UK reps ${q3_uk_hired_span}, and they serve different customers. The data shows a pattern; it cannot separate skill from territory.

Question 4 · Stock-out risk

Which products are at risk of stocking out, and are reorder levels set in line with how fast products actually sell?

Why it matters

When a product runs out, orders are lost or delayed, and fast sellers do the most damage. Northwind uses a simple rule to prevent this: each product has a reorder level, and when stock falls to that level, more is ordered from the supplier. The rule only works if reorder levels reflect how fast each product sells.

Approach

For the ${q4_n_active} active products (the ${q4_n_discontinued_word} discontinued ones are excluded), I first applied the reorder rule to current stock: is stock above the reorder level, and if not, is a restock already on order? I then added demand, the average units sold per month over the latest ${q4_n_months_word} full months (January–April ${q4_demand_year}), and calculated months of cover: units in stock plus units on order, divided by monthly demand. Products with less than ${q4_threshold_word} month of cover are flagged as at risk.

${q4_chart}

Points are spread slightly left and right so products with the same reorder level don't hide each other. Hover over a point for details.

Main finding

By its own rule, stock looks healthy: ${q4_ok} products are above their reorder level, ${q4_restock} have a restock on order, and only ${q4_missed_word} (${q4_missed_names}) was missed.

Against actual demand, it isn't: ${q4_at_risk} of ${q4_n_active} products have less than ${q4_threshold_word} month of cover, even counting stock on order, and ${q4_at_risk_pass} of them still pass the reorder rule. The rule is not catching the risk.

  • Reorder levels have no relationship to demand (rank correlation ${q4_corr}). Slow and fast sellers get similar reorder levels.
  • ${q4_zero} products have a reorder level of 0, so they can never trigger a reorder, and ${q4_zero_at_risk_word} of them are at risk. The worst case is ${q4_worst}: ${q4_worst_stock} units in stock, about ${q4_worst_demand} sold per month, roughly ${q4_worst_days} days of stock.
  • ${q4_key_product}, the single most important product (${q4_key_product_share} of all revenue), has about ${q4_key_product_cover_days} days of cover and nothing on order.
${q4_table}

The ${q4_table_rows_word} active products with the least cover. ${q4_no_sales_word} products with no sales in the period have no cover figure and are not counted as at risk.

Business implication

Reset reorder levels to match how fast products sell, for example at least one month of average demand. Start with the fast sellers that currently have a reorder level of 0, and with ${q4_key_product}. After that, review reorder levels regularly against recent sales, rather than leaving them fixed.

Caveat: the stock figures are a single, undated snapshot (assumed current as of ${data_end}), and stock is low relative to sales for most products. Read this as evidence that the reorder rules don't reflect demand, not as an exact forecast of which product runs out when.
