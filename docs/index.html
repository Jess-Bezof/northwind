Question 1 · Growth

Which markets and customers drove Northwind's growth from 1997 to 1998, and where should sales attention go next?

Why it matters

Northwind is growing quickly. Knowing exactly where that growth came from tells the sales team which markets and accounts to keep investing in, and which ones are slipping while the totals look healthy.

Approach

The data ends on 6 May 1998, so a full-year comparison would be misleading. Instead I compared the same four months, January–April 1997 vs January–April 1998, using revenue net of discounts, grouped by the customer's country. The chart shows the ten largest markets plus every market that declined (13 countries). For the three markets that grew most, I then broke the change down by customer.

Revenue by country, Jan–Apr 1997 vs Jan–Apr 1998

Hover over a bar for exact values. Numbers above the bars are the change in revenue.

Main finding

January–April revenue rose from $191k to $422k (+121%), and the number of orders went from 123 to 256.

The USA, Germany and Brazil delivered 57% of the growth, and in each of them one or two customers did most of the work:

  • USA (+$53.2k): Save-a-lot Markets and White Clover Markets, 60% of the country's growth.
  • Germany (+$47.4k): QUICK-Stop and Königlich Essen, 80% of the country's growth.
  • Brazil (+$31.7k): Hanari Carnes alone, 75% of the country's growth.

Sweden grew from $1.7k to $20.4k, about 12 times larger. France, a large market ($81k over the whole period), was nearly flat (+5%). Four markets shrank: Denmark (−$9.4k, −69%), Canada (−$3.7k, −24%), Mexico (−$3.1k, −44%), Finland (−$1.9k, −45%).

Business implication

Protect the accounts that are carrying the growth, and look hard at the markets that aren't growing. A handful of customers explain most of the increase, so account management for them should be a priority. Losing one would erase much of a market's gain (see Question 2). France and the four shrinking markets, especially Denmark, need a closer look from sales: find out whether customers are buying less or moving to competitors. Sweden shows that a small market can grow quickly with the right accounts.

Caveat: four months per year is a short window, and in small markets one large order can swing the result.

Question 2 · Customer dependence

Which markets depend on just one or two customers, and how much revenue is at risk if a key customer leaves?

Why it matters

Question 1 showed that growth often rests on a few accounts. If most of a country's revenue comes from one customer, that market is only as safe as that relationship: one lost account could wipe it out. Knowing where this risk sits tells the manager where to invest in keeping key accounts, and where to look for new customers.

Approach

For each of the 21 customer countries, using all orders from 4 Jul 1996 to 6 May 1998, I measured total revenue, the number of customers who ordered, and the share of revenue that came from the single largest customer. I call a market dependent when its largest customer brings in 50% or more of the country's revenue. "Revenue at risk" is that customer's revenue: what the market would lose if they left.

How dependent is each market on its largest customer? Bubble size and (n) = number of customers.

The shaded area marks dependent markets. The horizontal axis uses a log scale, because markets range from a few thousand to a few hundred thousand dollars. Hover over a bubble for details.

Main finding

12 of 21 markets depend on a single customer. Together they make up 35% of company revenue, and $316k of that sits with their top customers.

  • Austria is the clearest risk. It is Northwind's #3 market ($128k) but has only two customers, and Ernst Handel alone provides 82% ($105k).
  • Ireland, Norway and Poland each have just one customer. The largest of these is Ireland, where Hungry Owl All-Night Grocers is worth $50k.
  • France, the UK and Brazil are well diversified: 7–10 customers each, and no single customer above 30%.

A share-based rule misses the biggest dollar exposures. Germany and the USA fall just under the 50% line, so they are not flagged. Yet they hold Northwind's two largest single-customer exposures: QUICK-Stop in Germany ($110k, 49% of that market) and Save-a-lot Markets in the USA ($104k, 42% of that market).

Business implication

Treat the largest accounts as named relationships with an owner and a plan. Start with Ernst Handel in Austria, and with the top customers in Germany and the USA: these are the accounts whose loss would cost the most. In Austria and the one-customer markets, winning even one more sizeable customer would make the market far less fragile. The smallest one-customer markets are a low priority: little revenue depends on them.

Caveat: dependence is a risk, not a prediction. Nothing in the data suggests any of these customers is about to leave.

Question 3 · Sales team

Do top-performing sales reps sell more because they win more orders or bigger orders, and what does that suggest for coaching?

Why it matters

Revenue per sales rep varies widely, from $74k–$233k. Whether the best reps win on volume (more orders) or on size (bigger orders) points to different coaching: prospecting and follow-up in one case, upselling in the other.

Approach

Each rep's revenue equals their number of orders times their average order value. For the six sales representatives, I compared how much each of these two drivers varies across the team, and whether reps rank in the same order on each driver as they do on revenue. I left out the three employees in other roles, because they don't do the same job: Andrew Fuller (Vice President, Sales); Laura Callahan (Inside Sales Coordinator); Steven Buchanan (Sales Manager). Together they account for 29% of revenue.

Sales representatives: more orders or bigger orders? Bubble size = revenue; dotted curves = equal total revenue.

Reps on the same dotted curve have the same total revenue. Hover over a bubble for details.

Main finding

The top reps win more orders, not bigger ones. Across the team, the number of orders varies 3.6× (43–156), while average order value varies only 1.6× ($1,103–$1,798). Ranking reps by number of orders gives the same order as ranking them by revenue for 4 of the 6 reps; only Anne Dodsworth and Michael Suyama swap places. Order size does not follow revenue: Anne Dodsworth, who writes the largest orders, is 5th in revenue, and the top earner, Margaret Peacock, has only the 5th-largest average order.

  • The US office (Margaret Peacock, Janet Leverling and Nancy Davolio) handles 123–156 orders per rep. Margaret Peacock leads with 156 orders and $233k.
  • The UK office (Robert King, Anne Dodsworth and Michael Suyama) handles 43–72 orders per rep. Yet Anne Dodsworth and Robert King, both in the UK office, write the largest average orders on the team.
  • Michael Suyama is the exception: low on volume and the smallest average order ($1,103).

Business implication

Coaching should focus on winning more orders, especially in the UK office. The UK reps already write large orders, so the opportunity is to win more of them: more prospecting, more follow-up with existing customers, and learning from how the US reps generate volume. Michael Suyama would also benefit from help on order size. Revenue rankings should not be read as a measure of individual skill on their own (see the caveat).

Caveat: the office split also lines up with territory and seniority. The US reps were hired 1992–1993 and the UK reps 1993–1994, and they serve different customers. The data shows a pattern; it cannot separate skill from territory.

Question 4 · Stock-out risk

Which products are at risk of stocking out, and are reorder levels set in line with how fast products actually sell?

Why it matters

When a product runs out, orders are lost or delayed, and fast sellers do the most damage. Northwind uses a simple rule to prevent this: each product has a reorder level, and when stock falls to that level, more is ordered from the supplier. The rule only works if reorder levels reflect how fast each product sells.

Approach

For the 69 active products (the eight discontinued ones are excluded), I first applied the reorder rule to current stock: is stock above the reorder level, and if not, is a restock already on order? I then added demand, the average units sold per month over the latest four full months (January–April 1998), and calculated months of cover: units in stock plus units on order, divided by monthly demand. Products with less than one month of cover are flagged as at risk.

Reorder levels vs actual demand (69 active products)

Points are spread slightly left and right so products with the same reorder level don't hide each other. Hover over a point for details.

Main finding

By its own rule, stock looks healthy: 51 products are above their reorder level, 17 have a restock on order, and only one (Nord-Ost Matjeshering) was missed.

Against actual demand, it isn't: 30 of 69 products have less than one month of cover, even counting stock on order, and 29 of them still pass the reorder rule. The rule is not catching the risk.

  • Reorder levels have no relationship to demand (rank correlation −0.04). Slow and fast sellers get similar reorder levels.
  • 16 products have a reorder level of 0, so they can never trigger a reorder, and eight of them are at risk. The worst case is Camembert Pierrot: 19 units in stock, about 135 sold per month, roughly 4 days of stock.
  • Côte de Blaye, the single most important product (11% of all revenue), has about 8 days of cover and nothing on order.

Business implication

Reset reorder levels to match how fast products sell, for example at least one month of average demand. Start with the fast sellers that currently have a reorder level of 0, and with Côte de Blaye. After that, review reorder levels regularly against recent sales, rather than leaving them fixed.

Caveat: the stock figures are a single, undated snapshot (assumed current as of 6 May 1998), and stock is low relative to sales for most products. Read this as evidence that the reorder rules don't reflect demand, not as an exact forecast of which product runs out when.
